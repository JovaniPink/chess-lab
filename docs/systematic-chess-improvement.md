# Systematic Chess Improvement

## A rigorous framework for turning games into deliberate practice

> Chess improvement does not come primarily from playing more games or collecting more information. It comes from identifying the decisions you make badly, training the underlying skill deliberately, and testing the correction under serious playing conditions.

This guide is a practical coaching framework, not a controlled training study or a
promise of rating gains. The cycle lengths, time allocations, and rating bands below
are starting heuristics. Adjust them using the player's own error log, available time,
and tournament goals. Links to Lichess and Chess.com document available training tools
and workflows; they do not by themselves prove that one schedule works for every
player. The research claim in the final section is deliberately limited to the cited
Chase and Simon study.

## 1. The distinction between playing and studying

Many chess players plateau because they confuse **performing the game** with **training the skills that produce better performance**.

Playing is necessary. It exposes you to uncertainty, competitive pressure, time management, unfamiliar positions, and the psychological consequences of your decisions. But playing alone is an unreliable teacher. If you repeatedly make decisions using the same flawed habits, additional games merely give those habits more repetitions.

Fast chess intensifies this problem. Blitz and bullet rely heavily on patterns, intuitions, and heuristics already available to you. They can sharpen known patterns, improve practical resourcefulness, rehearse familiar openings, and provide enjoyment. But they usually leave too little time to construct a new calculation process or correct a weak one.

The accurate conclusion is not that fast chess inevitably destroys your chess. It is that **fast chess is usually a poor primary training environment for building deliberate calculation and evaluation habits**.

Systematic improvement requires a closed feedback loop:

1. Play a serious game.
2. Record what you thought during the game.
3. Analyze the game without computer assistance.
4. Identify the first important error—not merely the final blunder.
5. Diagnose why the decision failed.
6. Create a targeted exercise addressing that failure.
7. Test the correction in later games.

Without diagnosis and correction, chess activity can feel productive while producing little durable improvement.

### Knowledge versus execution

A developing player may already know that they should:

- Control the center.
- Develop pieces efficiently.
- Castle before beginning a premature attack.
- Place rooks on open files.
- Avoid unnecessary pawn weaknesses.
- Look for forcing moves.
- Activate the king in the endgame.

Yet knowledge at the verbal level does not guarantee execution over the board. The practical questions are:

- Do you notice that your opponent's last move removed a defender?
- Do you generate more than one candidate move?
- Do you calculate the opponent's strongest reply rather than the reply you expect?
- Do you continue a variation until the position becomes quiet enough to evaluate?
- Do you allocate time according to the importance of the decision?
- Do you recognize when the position has changed character?

Improvement is the conversion of chess knowledge into dependable decision-making.

## 2. Tactical Pattern Recognition and the Woodpecker Method

At developing and intermediate levels, tactics decide a large share of games. Even when a game appears to be lost strategically, the decisive event is often concrete: a fork, an overloaded defender, a back-rank weakness, a zwischenzug, or a piece left loose.

Random tactical puzzles are useful, but a purely random diet has limitations. You may solve a motif once, understand the answer, and then fail to recognize the same geometry several weeks later. Recognition becomes durable through correct repetition.

### Pattern recognition versus calculation

Tactical training serves two related purposes:

1. **Recognition:** Seeing familiar geometry quickly.
2. **Calculation:** Verifying that the apparent tactic works against the best defense.

Recognition tells you where to look. Calculation determines whether the idea is sound.

Strong tactical training must develop both. A player who calculates without recognizing patterns wastes time rediscovering basic motifs. A player who recognizes motifs without calculating becomes vulnerable to unsound sacrifices and superficial combinations.

### The Woodpecker Method

The Woodpecker Method, associated with Grandmaster Axel Smith and International Master Hans Tikkanen, uses repeated cycles through a fixed collection of tactical positions. Its central insight is simple: solve the same positions repeatedly, with progressively greater speed and accuracy, until recurring tactical geometries become familiar.

The following is a practical adaptation for an adult player with limited weekly time.

#### Step 1: Curate a fixed set

Choose approximately 200–500 exercises.

The set should be:

- Mostly solvable at your current level.
- Difficult enough to require attention.
- Broad enough to include multiple motifs.
- Free from an excessive number of composed or unrealistic positions.
- Stable across all cycles.

A useful distribution is:

| Puzzle type                               | Approximate share |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------: |
| Hanging pieces and simple captures        |               10% |
| Forks and double attacks                  |               15% |
| Pins and skewers                          |               10% |
| Removing or overloading defenders         |               15% |
| Discovered attacks and intermediate moves |               10% |
| Back-rank and mating patterns             |               15% |
| Defensive tactics                         |               10% |
| Mixed combinations                        |               15% |

#### Step 2: Complete the calculation cycle

During the first cycle:

1. Do not guess.
2. Identify the material balance and king safety.
3. List forcing candidates: checks, captures, and direct threats.
4. Calculate the opponent's strongest response.
5. Continue until the position becomes quiet.
6. Evaluate the final material and positional result.
7. Record whether you solved the position correctly.

The first cycle may take several weeks. Its purpose is accurate calculation and understanding.

#### Step 3: Repeat the identical set

After completing the set, begin it again. Do not replace the exercises with new ones merely because you remember some answers. Remembering is part of the intended training effect.

Across successive cycles, aim to:

- Reduce total completion time.
- Maintain or improve accuracy.
- Recognize the motif earlier.
- Recall the critical defensive response.
- Explain why tempting alternatives fail.

An adapted cycle schedule might be:

| Cycle | Completion window | Primary emphasis              |
| ----- | ----------------: | ----------------------------- |
| 1     |        21–28 days | Complete calculation          |
| 2     |           14 days | Faster candidate generation   |
| 3     |            7 days | Recognition plus verification |
| 4     |            4 days | Rapid retrieval               |
| 5     |            2 days | High accuracy under pace      |
| 6     |             1 day | Consolidation                 |

Do not force the schedule if accuracy collapses. Speed is valuable only after correctness.

### How to review a failed puzzle

Classify the failure:

- **Recognition failure:** You never considered the tactical idea.
- **Calculation failure:** You found the idea but calculated it incorrectly.
- **Visualization failure:** You lost track of a piece or square in the imagined position.
- **Defense failure:** You assumed a cooperative response.
- **Evaluation failure:** You calculated the line but misjudged the result.
- **Impulsiveness:** You entered a plausible move before completing the line.

The classification matters. Solving more random puzzles does not necessarily repair a visualization or evaluation problem.

### When not to use the Woodpecker Method

It should not consume your entire training program. Repeated tactical sets do not replace:

- Serious games.
- Calculation of unfamiliar positions.
- Endgame training.
- Strategic model games.
- Analysis of your own decisions.

Use repetition to automate foundational motifs while preserving separate sessions for novel calculation.

## 3. Calculation: Pattern Recognition Is Not Enough

Calculation is the disciplined examination of concrete variations. It is not the attempt to see every legal move. It is the process of generating meaningful candidates, testing them against the opponent's strongest replies, and comparing the resulting positions.

### A calculation protocol

For a critical position:

1. **Identify the opponent's threat.**
2. **Assess forcing features.** Locate checks, captures, loose pieces, exposed kings, pins, and overloaded defenders.
3. **Generate two or three candidates.** Include forcing, positional, and defensive options when appropriate.
4. **Calculate one branch at a time.** Avoid jumping repeatedly between variations.
5. **Assume best defense.** Do not calculate only the response you hope to see.
6. **Continue until quiet.** Stop when the tactical sequence has ended and the resulting position can be evaluated.
7. **Compare outcomes.** Consider material, king safety, piece activity, pawn structure, and initiative.
8. **Perform a final blunder check.** Visualize the selected move on the board and inspect the opponent's checks, captures, and threats.

### The most common calculation defects

#### Stopping one move too early

The player sees a fork, wins an exchange in imagination, and stops calculating before noticing the opponent's counterattack.

**Correction:** Continue until neither side has a forcing continuation.

#### Calculating only your own idea

The player asks, "How can I execute my attack?" but not, "What is the strongest action available to my opponent?"

**Correction:** After every candidate move, deliberately search for the opponent's most forcing response.

#### Failing to update the board

The player mentally leaves a captured piece on the board, forgets an opened diagonal, or imagines a pawn still defending a square.

**Correction:** At each ply, restate what changed: captured piece, opened line, altered defender, new king square.

#### Excessive breadth

The player considers six or seven candidates superficially and calculates none accurately.

**Correction:** Generate broadly, shortlist ruthlessly, calculate deeply.

#### Excessive depth in the wrong line

The player spends time analyzing an attractive continuation while ignoring a simple refutation.

**Correction:** Test candidates first against checks, captures, and direct tactical objections.

### Written calculation training

Twice per week, select one difficult position and spend 10–20 minutes analyzing it without moving the pieces. Write:

- Your evaluation of the initial position.
- Two or three candidate moves.
- The main line for each candidate.
- The final evaluation of each line.
- Your selected move.

Only afterward compare your work with an engine or annotated solution. Written analysis exposes vague visualization and unspoken assumptions.

## 4. Brutal Self-Analysis and the No-Silicon Rule

The most important material for your improvement is your own serious games. They reveal which mistakes survive contact with uncertainty, pressure, and the clock.

The **no-silicon rule** does not mean refusing to use engines. It means postponing engine assistance until after you have reconstructed and tested your own thinking.

### Why immediate engine review can fail

The common workflow is:

1. Finish a game.
2. Open the engine.
3. Find the largest evaluation swing.
4. Observe the recommended move.
5. Assume the mistake is understood.
6. Start another game.

This produces information without diagnosis. The engine may show that `21...Re3` wins, but it does not automatically tell you why you failed to consider rook lifts, why you underestimated the attack, or why you spent eight minutes calculating an irrelevant capture.

### Phase 1: Capture your memory

Immediately after the game, record:

- Where you left known opening territory.
- Where you first felt uncertain.
- Which moves consumed significant time.
- Candidate moves you considered.
- Variations you calculated.
- Where you believed each side was better.
- Moments when your emotional state changed.
- Your remaining time at critical moments.

The engine can reconstruct the position. It cannot reconstruct your original thought process.

### Phase 2: Analyze without assistance

Replay the game and annotate:

1. The first unfamiliar position.
2. The first difficult decision.
3. The first meaningful deterioration.
4. The final decisive mistake.
5. Missed opportunities for both sides.
6. Strategic plans considered or ignored.
7. Endgame transitions.

Do not analyze only losses. Wins can contain poor decisions that went unpunished, and draws can reveal missed wins or strong defensive resources.

### Phase 3: Diagnose the human failure

For every major error, ask:

- Did I notice the opponent's threat?
- Did I generate enough candidates?
- Did I calculate the opponent's best defense?
- Did I stop the line too early?
- Did I visualize the resulting board incorrectly?
- Did I calculate correctly but evaluate incorrectly?
- Did I misunderstand the pawn structure?
- Was the decision distorted by time pressure, impatience, fear, or overconfidence?

The purpose is to find the **cause of the move**, not merely the replacement move.

### Phase 4: Perform the silicon check

Only after completing human analysis should you use Stockfish or another strong engine.

Use it to:

- Test your candidate moves.
- Refute incomplete variations.
- Discover defensive resources.
- Check tactical claims.
- Compare two plausible plans.
- Verify theoretical endgames.

Do not simply accept the top line. Explore alternatives and ask what feature makes the engine move work. An engine is an exceptionally strong fact-checker, but a weak teacher unless you interrogate its conclusions.

### Phase 5: Extract one to three lessons

Each game should produce a small number of actionable lessons, such as:

- Scan what a moved piece stopped defending.
- Do not open the center while behind in development.
- In this structure, prepare `...c5` before beginning queenside operations.
- Preserve the active rook rather than exchanging automatically.
- Calculate intermediate checks before assuming a recapture.

If your analysis produces twenty disconnected lessons, you have not prioritized effectively.

## 5. Endgame Superiority Over Opening Memorization

José Raúl Capablanca famously argued that players should study the endgame first because endings can be studied independently, while openings and middlegames must ultimately be understood in relation to the ending.

The practical force of this advice remains strong. Opening memorization is fragile. Endgame understanding transfers across openings and teaches exact properties of the pieces.

Endgame study develops:

- Precise calculation.
- Visualization of pawn races.
- King activity.
- Piece coordination.
- Understanding of exchanges.
- Recognition of favorable simplifications.
- Conversion of material advantages.
- Defensive resourcefulness.

### Priority 1: Basic checkmates

Master:

- King and queen versus king.
- King and rook versus king.
- Ladder mate.
- Basic queen-and-rook mating patterns.

These teach restriction, coordination, and avoidance of stalemate.

### Priority 2: Pawn endings

Know:

- The rule of the square.
- Key squares.
- Direct and distant opposition.
- Triangulation.
- Zugzwang.
- Protected and outside passed pawns.
- Basic pawn races.
- When a pawn breakthrough works.

Pawn endings are unforgiving. One tempo can change a win into a draw or a draw into a loss. That precision makes them ideal calculation training.

### Priority 3: Rook endings

Master these principles and anchors:

- Activate the rook.
- Place the rook behind a passed pawn when circumstances permit.
- Cut off the opposing king.
- Prefer active counterplay to passive waiting.
- Learn the Lucena position and the bridge-building method.
- Learn the Philidor position and the sixth-rank defense.
- Understand checking distance.
- Recognize when exchanging rooks enters a favorable pawn ending.

The value of theoretical anchors is not rote knowledge alone. If you recognize that a transition leads to a known win or draw, you can calculate toward it with confidence.

### Priority 4: Minor-piece endings

Study:

- Good bishop versus bad bishop.
- Bishop versus knight imbalances.
- Opposite-colored bishop drawing mechanisms.
- Same-colored bishop weaknesses.
- Knight blockades.
- Fixed pawns and color complexes.
- Creating a second weakness.

### Train execution, not recognition

Knowing what Lucena means is not the same as being able to win the position under resistance.

Use this protocol:

1. Learn the principle.
2. Reconstruct the position from memory.
3. Play it against an engine or partner.
4. Switch sides and defend it.
5. Repeat it after several days.
6. Test a nearby position in which the same principle applies imperfectly.

## 6. Structural Opening Study

Opening study becomes useful when it prepares you for the middlegame. The objective is not to reproduce a move sequence mechanically; it is to reach positions whose plans, pawn breaks, tactical themes, and endgames you understand.

### Learn openings in four layers

#### Layer 1: Principles and purpose

Explain what the opening is trying to accomplish:

- How does each side contest the center?
- Where will the king usually go?
- Which pieces are easy or difficult to develop?
- What imbalance is each side accepting?

#### Layer 2: Pawn structure

Identify:

- Open and semi-open files.
- Pawn majorities.
- Isolated, backward, doubled, or hanging pawns.
- Strong outposts.
- Weak color complexes.
- Typical pawn breaks.

#### Layer 3: Model games

Study complete games illustrating:

- A typical attack.
- A typical positional plan.
- A thematic pawn break.
- A characteristic favorable exchange.
- A common defensive method.
- A typical endgame.

#### Layer 4: Concrete variations

Memorize lines when your actual games demonstrate that precision is necessary. Add branches incrementally rather than importing a large theoretical tree you rarely encounter.

### Example: The Carlsbad structure

The Carlsbad structure most famously arises from the Exchange Variation of the Queen's Gambit Declined. Its asymmetric pawn skeleton gives White several recurring plans.

#### The minority attack

White advances the queenside pawns, often with `b4–b5`, to provoke or create a weakness in Black's queenside pawn chain. The objective is not necessarily to win a pawn immediately; it is to create a durable target, often on `c6`.

#### The central break

White may prepare `e3–e4`, using central expansion to create more active piece play. This requires careful preparation because the break changes the center and may expose new tactical lines.

#### Kingside expansion

White can sometimes place a knight on `e5`, support it with `f2–f4`, and develop attacking prospects on the kingside.

The important lesson is not that one plan is always correct. Structural understanding gives you a menu of plans and the ability to choose according to the actual placement of the pieces.

### Maintain a narrow repertoire

You need:

- One primary first move as White.
- One response to `1.e4`.
- One response to `1.d4`.
- Basic treatment of sidelines.

For each opening, keep a one-page summary containing:

- Central idea.
- Typical pawn structure.
- Ideal piece placement.
- Standard pawn breaks.
- Common tactical motifs.
- Favorable and unfavorable exchanges.
- Three to five model games.
- Lines that have actually occurred in your games.

## 7. Time-Control Discipline

To build calculation habits, play games that permit actual calculation.

### Recommended training controls

- **15+10:** Practical minimum for a serious online training game.
- **30+20:** Strong default for deliberate online improvement.
- **45+15 or 60+10:** Better for deep calculation and tournament preparation.
- **Classical over-the-board:** Best environment for sustained concentration and full post-game reconstruction.

Increment is valuable because it protects the educational value of the endgame. A thoughtful game should not automatically become a mouse-speed contest after move 35.

### A balanced interpretation of fast chess

Blitz and bullet can help with:

- Opening repetition.
- Pattern retrieval.
- Practical defense.
- Clock handling.
- Enjoyment and motivation.

They are poor substitutes for slow games when the training target is:

- Deep calculation.
- Candidate comparison.
- Positional evaluation.
- Endgame technique.
- Construction of a new thinking routine.

A reasonable rule is to treat blitz as dessert rather than the main course.

### Allocate time by decision importance

Spend little time when a move is forced or familiar. Spend more when:

- The center may open.
- A pawn break is available.
- A major exchange changes the character of the position.
- A sacrifice is possible.
- King safety changes.
- You must choose between incompatible plans.
- The game may transition into an endgame.
- Your opponent makes a surprising move.

Do not spend six minutes seeking perfection in an ordinary position and then have thirty seconds for the decisive calculation.

## 8. A Disciplined In-Game Thinking Process

### After every opposing move

Ask:

1. What changed?
2. What is threatened?
3. What did the moved piece begin attacking?
4. What did it stop defending?
5. Which file, rank, or diagonal opened or closed?
6. Are any pieces loose, pinned, or overloaded?

### In a routine position

Use a short scan:

1. Address the opponent's threat.
2. Identify your worst-placed piece.
3. Look for an improving move or thematic pawn break.
4. Check that the intended move is tactically safe.

### In a critical position

Use the full process:

1. **Threat:** What does the opponent want?
2. **Evaluation:** Material, king safety, activity, pawn structure, space, and initiative.
3. **Candidates:** Two or three serious options.
4. **Calculation:** Best response to each candidate.
5. **Comparison:** Which resulting position is preferable and why?
6. **Blunder check:** Opponent's checks, captures, and threats after the chosen move.

### Before releasing the piece

Visualize the move as already played and ask:

- Is the destination square safe?
- What checks does the opponent have?
- What captures does the opponent have?
- What direct threats does the opponent have?
- What did my moved piece stop defending?
- Did I expose my king or queen?
- Is there an intermediate move before the expected recapture?

This final check should be brief but mandatory.

## 9. Converting Mistakes Into Training

Maintain an error log. For every serious game, classify the most important errors.

| Error category        | Typical symptom                        | Corrective training                           |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Threat blindness      | Missed what the last move attacked     | Mandatory post-move threat scan               |
| Tactical recognition  | Never considered the motif             | Themed pattern repetition                     |
| Calculation depth     | Stopped one move early                 | Written long-line calculation                 |
| Calculation breadth   | Considered only one candidate          | Candidate-generation drill                    |
| Visualization         | Misplaced or forgot a piece mentally   | No-board calculation with board-state updates |
| Evaluation            | Saw the line but judged it incorrectly | Compare resulting positions and model games   |
| Strategic planning    | Had no meaningful plan                 | Pawn-structure study                          |
| Opening understanding | Remembered moves but not ideas         | Model games and structural notes              |
| Endgame technique     | Did not know the method                | Reconstruct and play the position repeatedly  |
| Time management       | Entered critical play with no time     | Clock checkpoints and decision triage         |
| Psychology            | Played impulsively after a mistake     | Reset routine and defensive calculation       |

### The one-game, one-drill rule

Every analyzed game should produce at least one specific training action.

Examples:

- Missed a back-rank tactic → solve 20 back-rank positions.
- Misplayed an isolated queen's pawn → study three IQP model games.
- Failed to convert rook and extra pawn → practice the resulting ending from both sides.
- Spent too long in the opening → establish move-10 and move-20 clock checkpoints.

This rule closes the gap between analysis and behavior change.

## 10. A Sustainable Weekly Program

For approximately five hours per week:

| Activity             |        Frequency |     Weekly time |
| -------------------- | ---------------: | --------------: |
| Tactical repetition  |   5 × 15 minutes |      75 minutes |
| Novel calculation    |   2 × 20 minutes |      40 minutes |
| Serious games        | 2 games at 30+20 | 100–140 minutes |
| Human-first analysis |   2 × 30 minutes |      60 minutes |
| Endgame execution    |   2 × 20 minutes |      40 minutes |
| Model game           |        1 session |      30 minutes |
| Opening maintenance  |  1 short session |      20 minutes |

The exact total varies with game length. Protect these priorities:

1. Serious games and analysis.
2. Tactics and calculation.
3. Endgame execution.
4. Model games.
5. Opening maintenance.

If your error log reveals a clear bottleneck, adjust the allocation. A player repeatedly losing rook endings should temporarily spend more time on rook endings. A player hanging pieces should not respond by learning twelve additional opening variations.

## 11. A Twelve-Week Improvement Cycle

### Weeks 1–2: Baseline and threat awareness

- Play four serious games.
- Analyze each without an engine.
- Start an error log.
- Use the question "What changed?" after every opposing move.
- Record unforced material losses.

### Weeks 3–4: Tactical foundations

- Begin the fixed tactical set.
- Emphasize forks, pins, skewers, loose pieces, and removal of defenders.
- Perform a blunder check before every move.
- Continue serious games and human-first analysis.

### Weeks 5–6: Calculation discipline

- Add two written calculation sessions per week.
- Generate at least two candidates in every critical position.
- Calculate the opponent's best defense.
- Track lines stopped too early.

### Weeks 7–8: Endgame foundations

- Practice opposition, key squares, pawn races, Lucena, and Philidor.
- Play each theoretical position from both sides.
- Review every endgame transition from recent games.

### Weeks 9–10: Pawn structures and model games

- Select one recurring structure from your repertoire.
- Study three complete annotated games.
- Record typical pawn breaks, piece placement, and favorable exchanges.
- Explain your middlegame plan after each serious game.

### Week 11: Opening consolidation

- Create one-page summaries for your main openings.
- Add only variations arising from your games.
- Attach model games to each structural family.
- Remove memorized branches you cannot explain.

### Week 12: Audit and redesign

Review the complete cycle:

- Serious games played.
- Games analyzed without an engine first.
- Unforced piece losses.
- Missed opponent threats.
- Calculation failures.
- Time-trouble frequency.
- Endgames converted or saved.
- Repeated error categories.
- Rating trend as a secondary measure.

Build the next cycle around the largest remaining bottleneck.

## 12. How to Measure Real Progress

Rating matters, but it is a lagging and noisy measure. Track leading indicators that reflect decision quality.

### Recommended metrics

- Serious games completed.
- Percentage analyzed without an engine first.
- Unforced piece losses per game.
- Missed immediate threats per game.
- Critical positions with multiple candidates considered.
- Games entering severe time trouble.
- Theoretical endgames executed correctly.
- Tactical-set accuracy and completion time.
- Frequency of repeated error categories.
- Number of analyzed games converted into specific drills.

### A better initial goal

Do not begin with "gain 200 rating points." Begin with:

> Play and analyze 24 serious games, reduce unforced tactical losses, and measurably improve the two decision errors that appear most frequently in the first six games.

Rating improvement should emerge from improved decisions rather than serve as the only evidence of them.

---

## 13. What to Stop Doing

- Playing large volumes of bullet and calling it training.
- Turning on the engine before recording your own thoughts.
- Memorizing a new opening after every loss.
- Guessing tactical solutions.
- Watching instructional videos without pausing to predict moves.
- Studying advanced positional concepts while routinely hanging pieces.
- Changing repertoires before learning the resulting pawn structures.
- Measuring study by hours consumed rather than errors corrected.
- Treating engine accuracy as a complete evaluation of human decision quality.
- Playing serious games while distracted, exhausted, or unwilling to analyze afterward.

### Foundational research perspective

Chess expertise depends heavily on meaningful pattern recognition. Chase and Simon's classic study, [“Perception in Chess”](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90004-2>), helped establish that stronger players perceive structured relationships in realistic chess positions rather than merely remembering isolated pieces. That finding supports the combined use of repeated tactical patterns, complete games, and structural study—but it does not eliminate the need for calculation.

## Final principle

The durable chess-improvement loop is:

> **Play slowly. Think explicitly. Analyze honestly. Diagnose precisely. Train specifically. Repeat.**

The purpose of study is not to know more chess facts. It is to make better decisions in positions you have never seen before.
